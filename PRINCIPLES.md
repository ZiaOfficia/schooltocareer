# Principles

Product principles, not technical ones. These exist to settle arguments between
two options that both look good.

---

### 1. Help the student finish the task

The measure of a page is whether someone can close the tab having done what they
came to do. Not time on page, not scroll depth — those go up when people are
confused.

### 2. Trust is more important than traffic

A wrong exam date costs a student a year. We would rather say "not announced yet"
than guess, and rather lose a click than earn it with a number we cannot source.

### 3. Original insight beats copied information

Every competitor has the notification PDF. What we can have, and they do not, is
what the data says across years. That is the only durable advantage.

### 4. Every page recommends the next useful step

A page that answers one question and stops has done half its job. The student
almost always has a next question, and we usually know what it is.

### 5. We build systems, not isolated pages

If a fix has to be applied to more than one page by hand, it is the wrong fix.
Templates, components, and one source of truth per fact.

### 6. Index quality matters more than URL count

Publishing a thin page is not neutral — it costs crawl budget and dilutes the
domain. A page that is not ready is `noindex` until it is.

### 7. Freshness is a feature

Exam content decays. A page that was correct in March and wrong in June is worse
than no page at all, because it was trusted. Maintenance is not overhead here;
it is the product.

### 8. Every feature should reduce student uncertainty

Before building anything, name the uncertainty it removes. If it does not remove
one, it is decoration — however elegant the implementation.

---

## How to use these

When two options are equally attractive, the one that serves the earlier
principle wins. Trust beats traffic. Finishing the task beats engagement.

They are also a veto. "It ranks well" does not override principle 2, and
"it's only one page" does not override principle 6.
